Street Fighter: Games, Characters, Story, and the Legacy of a Fighting Game Franchise

A clear starting point for curious newcomers and returning players: where Street Fighter began, what makes its matches work, and how its biggest games differ.

By MaxCinema Editorial • Updated {{ article_published }}

What is Street Fighter?

Street Fighter is Capcom’s long-running one-on-one fighting-game series. Two players—or one player and the computer—try to outthink each other through movement, timing, spacing, attacks, blocks, throws, and special moves. The round is short, but the decisions are dense: a step forward can threaten a throw, a jump can start an attack, and one blocked move can change who has the advantage.

The series began in arcades in 1987 with Ryu and Ken as the playable fighters. Its follow-up, Street Fighter II, broadened the roster and helped make character-specific moves, matchups, and combos central to the language of fighting games. Capcom’s own retrospective traces those early games and the techniques that still define Ryu and Ken’s style today.

The main games and how the series evolved

Era What it introduced or emphasized Why it matters
Street Fighter & II Distinct fighters, special-move motions, six attack buttons, and the competitive arcade matchup. It established the series’ vocabulary: fireballs, anti-airs, throws, charge moves, and rival characters.
Alpha & III Faster styles, expanded meter systems, and more expressive offensive and defensive choices. These games are touchstones for players who enjoy precise movement and long-term mastery.
IV & V A return to a 2D fighting plane with 3D visuals, followed by new character-specific system mechanics. They reintroduced Street Fighter to a large online and tournament audience while keeping its footsies-first identity.
Street Fighter 6 Fighting Ground, World Tour, and Battle Hub, plus Classic, Modern, and Dynamic control types. It gives new players more ways to learn while keeping traditional competitive play at the center.

These are not interchangeable sequels. Each entry has its own speed, systems, roster balance, and community history. If you are choosing one to play rather than studying the whole timeline, it is more useful to ask what kind of learning experience you want.

The characters at the heart of Street Fighter

The roster is a major reason the franchise lasts. A character is not only a look or backstory; they are also a playstyle. Ryu rewards steady, fundamental play. Ken usually turns a close-range opening into momentum. Chun-Li uses fast normals and careful space control. Guile asks players to manage charge timing and projectiles. Zangief changes the pace by making close range dangerous. Cammy, Juri, Luke, and many others offer different answers to the same question: how do you make the opponent hesitate?

The most useful way to choose a first character is practical: pick one whose movement and basic attacks feel understandable, then learn a small reliable game plan. A familiar face is a good reason to start, but consistency matters more than copying advanced combo videos.

How the story and world fit together

Street Fighter’s story is a shared world rather than one uninterrupted novel. Tournaments, training journeys, rivalries, criminal organizations, and city-level conflicts bring fighters together. Ryu’s search for self-mastery and his rivalry with Ken form a recurring emotional center, while Chun-Li, Guile, Cammy, and other fighters connect personal goals to larger threats.

The exact chronology can feel messy because games revisit periods, introduce side stories, and change emphasis from one entry to another. For a newcomer, the better approach is to learn each character’s motivation in the game you are playing first. The wider lore becomes easier to follow once the matchups and relationships are familiar.

The mechanics that make a Street Fighter match work

Neutral and spacing

Before a big combo, players fight over the space where their attacks can reach and the opponent’s cannot. Walking, crouching, and choosing when not to press a button are skills.

Anti-airs and jumps

A jump can begin pressure, but it is also a commitment. Learning one dependable anti-air attack is often more valuable than learning a difficult combo immediately.

Punishes and confirms

When an unsafe move misses or is blocked, a punish takes advantage of that opening. A confirm is recognizing that an attack connected before committing to a follow-up.

Meter and momentum

Supers and game-specific resources create bursts of power, but spending them at the right moment matters as much as earning them.

Street Fighter 6 also offers control schemes with different levels of input complexity. Modern controls simplify access to special moves, while Classic retains the traditional six-button layout and motion inputs. Neither replaces the need to read the opponent; they simply change how you execute your decisions.

What should a newcomer play first?

For most people who want a current, guided entry point, Street Fighter 6 is the clearest place to begin. Its tutorials, control options, training tools, World Tour mode, and active online community make it easier to experiment without pretending that the game has no depth. Start with a character you enjoy, try both Classic and Modern controls, and keep the first goal small: block a jump, land an anti-air, and use one simple punish.

If you want arcade history, a collection that includes the older games is a better fit. Street Fighter II is a useful lesson in foundational design; Alpha games highlight speed and style; Street Fighter III is often chosen by players who want to explore parry-focused play. Availability changes by platform and region, so check the official store page for the version you intend to buy.

Why the franchise still matters

Street Fighter endures because it is easy to understand at a glance and difficult to exhaust. Anyone can see a fighter jump, block, throw a fireball, or land a dramatic final hit. Under that surface is a conversation about risk: one player represents a threat, the other answers it, and both adapt. That clarity made arcade rivalries memorable and still makes a close modern match compelling.

Its legacy is not just a list of famous characters or tournament moments. It is the design idea that a fighting game can give each player clear tools, then create drama from how, when, and why those tools are used.
